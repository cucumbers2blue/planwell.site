Exploring digital citizenship (online safety, netiquette, protecting personal information, recognising online risks) alongside foundational computer systems (hardware vs software, input/output devices, storage devices), basic design principles and file management, and an introduction to coding (algorithms and block-based coding)—culminating in an MYP Design Cycle mini-project in which students design, create and evaluate a digital product addressing a real “digital world” need.

Inquiry

Key concept

  • Systems

Related concepts

  • Evaluation
  • Function

Global context

  • Identities and relationships

Statement of inquiry

Understanding how digital systems work helps individuals design ethical, responsible solutions that protect their identity and relationships online.

Inquiry questions

  • Factual: What is a digital citizen, and what are the main components of a computer system?
  • Conceptual: How does understanding a system's parts (hardware, software, input, output, storage) help us design safer and better digital products?
  • Debatable: Should young people have the same online rights and responsibilities as adults?

Approaches to learning

Self-management: organization skills

  • Managing time and tasks effectively.

Self-management skills are taught during Weeks 10–13 (Design Foundations), where file management and folder/naming conventions are framed as personal organisation tools, and again in Weeks 14–18, where students pace their own mini-project against a simple plan across research, design, creation and evaluation, revisiting progress at set checkpoints.

Thinking: critical-thinking skills

  • Analysing and evaluating issues and ideas.

Thinking skills are explicitly taught during Weeks 5–9 (Computer Systems), where students break down how a digital system works into its component parts, and again in Weeks 14–18, where algorithmic thinking (sequencing steps in block-based coding) is connected back to design planning from Weeks 10–13—making the transfer between topics visible and named.

Learner profile

  • Thinkers
  • Principled

MYP Design aims

  • Enjoy the design process and develop an appreciation of its elegance and power.
  • Develop an appreciation of the impact of design innovations for life, global society and environments.
  • Develop respect for others' viewpoints and appreciate alternative solutions to problems.

Objectives

Criterion A: Inquiring and analysing

1. Explain and justify the need for a solution to a problem.

2. State and prioritize the main points of research needed to develop a solution to the problem.

3. Describe the main features of an existing product that inspires a solution to the problem.

4. Present the main findings of relevant research.

Criterion B: Developing ideas

1. Develop a list of success criteria for the solution.

2. Present feasible design ideas that can be correctly interpreted by others.

3. Present the chosen design.

4. Create a planning drawing or diagram that outlines the main details for making the chosen solution.

Criterion C: Creating the solution

1. Outline a plan that considers the use of resources and time and is sufficient for peers to follow to create the solution.

2. Demonstrate excellent technical skills when making the solution.

3. Follow the plan to create the solution so that it functions as intended.

4. List the changes made to the chosen design and plan when making the solution.

Criterion D: Evaluating

1. Outline simple, relevant testing methods that generate data to measure the success of the solution.

2. Outline the success of the solution against the design specification.

3. Outline how the solution could be improved.

4. Outline the impact of the solution on the client or target audience.

Assessment

The mini-project assesses all four MYP Design criteria:

  • Criterion A: Research into a real digital-world problem.
  • Criterion B: The design specification and plan.
  • Criterion C: The completed digital artefact.
  • Criterion D: Testing and evaluation against the design specification.

Language and literacy development

This unit supports language development through new vocabulary related to digital citizenship, computer systems, design and coding—for example: digital footprint, netiquette, hardware, software, algorithm, and file format.

Students will practise:

  • Reading comprehension through scenario cards.
  • Speaking and listening through class discussions.
  • Clear written communication through their design specification and process journal.

Differentiation

  • Extended time for students who need it to complete research, planning or product creation.
  • One-to-one supervision and check-ins for students needing additional support.
  • Choice of product format—poster, simple coded program or digital artefact—based on students' strengths and interests.

Co-curricular and Service as Action links

Students may design their digital product to address a real need within their school or family community—for example, a simple guide or product helping younger students understand safe file organisation or online safety.

This reinforces the unit's link to Service as Action by connecting the mini-project to an authentic audience beyond the classroom.
